The macro odds, ▮ priced in real time

Market-implied probabilities for the events that drive the quarterly heat map — the next FOMC decision, the funds-rate level after it, CPI inflation, GDP growth, the unemployment rate, and NBER recession calls — plus macro tail risks, the IPO pipeline, and the full implied probability distributions behind the headline numbers. Every number is the daily closing price of a $1 binary contract on a federally regulated exchange, read directly as a probability. All charts share one fixed 0–1 axis; dashed verticals mark resolution days; gaps where no market is listed are marked, never interpolated.

Methodology: reading event-market prices as probabilities

Prices are probabilities. Contracts pay $1 if the stated outcome occurs, so a daily close of $0.86 is a 0.86 probability. Nothing is transformed, smoothed, or annualized: charts draw straight lines between daily closes, and days without trades carry the exchange's previous close (volume 0 in the committed archive).

What is charted. Most series chart the front market — the nearest open event, discovered dynamically, never a hardcoded calendar. The recession series charts the full annual ladder: every open calendar-year market. Dashed verticals mark scheduled resolution — meeting day, release day, or calendar year-end for the ladder. Every chart uses the same fixed 0–1 probability axis, so levels are comparable across series.

Reading the strikes. Threshold strikes like “≥4.0%” are cumulative and not mutually exclusive — a full ladder spans the distribution — while decision buckets use the exchange's own mutually exclusive wording. Settled events drop out of discovery but stay in the archive; listed-print gaps are noted, never interpolated.

Implied distributions. For the threshold series (fed funds, CPI, GDP, unemployment) each day's strike ladder is converted into a full implied probability distribution — bin probabilities by strike differencing with mode-outward monotonicity, following Section 3 of the paper — rendered as a fan chart (median with 50% and 90% intervals) and a density heatmap. These are risk-neutral probabilities, not physical ones, and thin tail contracts can be stale. The same distributions feed the macro tail-risk series (Figure 13 of the paper) and the daily mean/variance uncertainty gauge.

These markets are documented as a high-frequency, continuously updated, distributionally rich benchmark of macroeconomic expectations in Finance and Economics Discussion Series 2026-010, “Kalshi and the Rise of Macro Markets”, Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, https://doi.org/10.17016/FEDS.2026.010.
