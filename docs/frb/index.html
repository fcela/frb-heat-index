Mapping Heat in the U.S. Financial System

A replication of the Federal Reserve's heat map of financial-system vulnerabilities, rebuilt indicator-by-indicator from public data. Forty-eight indicators feed fourteen components across three vulnerability categories — every cell below is a quantile in its own history. Blue is subdued, red is acute.

Beyond the 44 indicators in the FEDS Notes version, this implementation includes the two additional indicators from the final journal publication (J. Banking & Finance 81, 2017): total SRISK relative to total assets and significant Granger-causality connections among large financial institutions.

The heat map

Fourteen components × quarters. Colors are quantiles of each component's own historical distribution — a red cell means "hotter than most of this component's history," not an absolute level. Hover any cell for the exact value.

Reading the scale: 0.00 is the lowest vulnerability reading on record for that component (deep blue); 1.00 is the highest (deep red). Low values are information, not errors — gray cells are the ones with no observation.

The heat map, clustered

The same heat, resolved to its 48 indicators and re-ordered by shape: rows are the indicators, columns are every quarter in order — time is never re-shuffled. The row order comes from hierarchical clustering of the score trajectories, so indicators that heat up and cool down together sit next to each other; the dendrogram at the left shows the merge tree. The chip on each label is the indicator's component — compare it across rows to see where the clustering recovers the paper's fourteen components and where it cuts across them. Hover any cell for the component, sub-index, and exact value.

Method: what is clustered is the 48 indicator score series (each indicator's 0–1 heat quantile in its own history, per quarter). Distance is correlation distance (1 − Pearson r) computed on pairwise-complete observations — at least 24 shared quarters, no imputation, no zero-filling; linkage is average (UPGMA), because Ward's objective requires complete raw vectors and cannot take the ragged sample. Adjacent rows mean similar vulnerability trajectories, not similar levels. The order and merge tree are computed deterministically in the data pipeline and shipped with the site data, never in the browser.

Methodology

Every indicator passes through the same six-step pipeline, documented formula-by-formula in the replication paper (fed_heat_map-replication.pdf).

  1. Sign orientation.

    Each series is flipped as needed so that higher values always mean greater vulnerability. Compressed credit spreads and low volatility read as complacency; high capital ratios, high saving rates, and tightening lending standards read as safer.

  2. Transform.

    Natural log for strictly positive, right-skewed series (spreads, volatility, issuance); a 10-year trailing moving-average detrending for trending ratio-to-GDP series, so the index reacts to the cyclical component; no transform otherwise.

  3. Standardize.

    Z-scores using each indicator's full-sample mean and standard deviation.

  4. Component index.

    Simple average of the standardized indicators within each component.

  5. Heat CDF.

    Each component is mapped to (0, 1) through a Gaussian kernel CDF with Silverman bandwidth, evaluated at each observation. This is what makes every heat-map cell a historical quantile — colors are relative to history, never absolute levels.

  6. Aggregate index.

    The mean of the 14 component quantiles, mapped through its own kernel CDF again → the 0–1 headline index.

Honest caveats (from the replication paper)

  • Commercial real estate is a pipeline discrepancy, not a data gap. The documented pipeline cannot reproduce the paper's CRE component even on the paper's own data — a finding about the paper itself.
  • The maturity gap is a fitted proxy, not a direct measurement. The weighted-average maturity gap at commercial banks is rebuilt by OLS regression of the authors' accessible series on the 10-year Treasury yield, VIX, and the loans-to-deposits ratio (R² 0.80, RMSE 1.8 months); out-of-sample quarters rely on the stability of the 1997–2010 relationship.
  • Extending the sample moves history. Standardization, the 10-year detrending, and the kernel-CDF bandwidth are all computed over the full available sample, so values for early quarters shift as new quarters are added. The extended map is the same methodology on a longer sample — not point-for-point comparable with the paper's 2015 map.
  • Coverage: 48 of 48 indicators from named public sources — all 44 FEDS Notes indicators, both journal-publication additions (SRISK and Granger-causality connectedness), and two hedge-fund leverage series added in 2026 (OFR Form PF aggregates and the SCOOS dealer survey, both in Nonbank leverage); in the replication paper the public-proxy panel tracks the paper's published aggregate index at correlation 0.93.
  • Recent quarters are nowcast. Source-release lags leave 32 series ending at 2025Q4; 2026Q1–Q2 values are filled by tested nowcast models or documented assumptions — every filled value is flagged in data/nowcast_provenance.csv, and the panel of observed values alone remains available as data/panel_quarterly.csv.
